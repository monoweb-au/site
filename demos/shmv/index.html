Fusion Fight & Fitness

2014 – 2017 · The beginning

It started in 2014 when David D'Amore packed up from Melbourne — where he'd spent four years working across gyms, exploring every training style he could get his hands on — and landed in Cairns. He set up inside Fusion Fight & Fitness, a BJJ and Muay Thai gym, and started running classes that didn't look like anything else on the timetable.

Shoshin had its own dedicated space within Fusion — a section of the gym with a mural on the wall marking it as theirs. The sessions blended gymnastics strength with handstand work, ground movement with Olympic weightlifting and kettlebell work, crawling patterns with mobility drills. People would show up not quite sure what they were signing up for. That was part of it.

Word got around. Outside of the regular Shoshin members, some of the BJJ and Muay Thai fighters started turning up to Dave's classes to sharpen their strength and mobility — recognising that the way he trained could give them an edge on the mats and in the ring. It was the first sign that the method had crossover appeal beyond the movement crowd.

By late 2014, the classes had a name. David chose Shoshin — from the Zen concept of shoshin (初心), meaning beginner's mind. The idea that no matter how experienced you are, you show up to every session with the openness of someone learning for the first time. No egos. No fixed identity. Just curiosity about what your body can do today.

The early crew at Fusion was small — a handful of regulars training barefoot alongside combat athletes, building something nobody in Cairns had seen before. There was no playbook for what Shoshin was doing. Nobody else in the city was running a movement studio. Nobody was teaching structured handstand progressions or strength-based flexibility work in a group class format. It was new, and the people who found it knew they'd found something different.

Three years at Fusion proved the demand was real. What started as a coach with a method had become a community — and it needed a home of its own.

McLeod Street Studio

2018 – 2019 · The first home

In 2018, Shoshin moved out of the fight gym and into its own space — a 75-square-metre shopfront at Pyramid Plaza on McLeod Street. It was modest by any measure. But for the first time, the space was theirs, and the sign on the building said Shoshin Movement.

That distinction mattered. Having a standalone studio meant a full timetable and the ability to properly shape the environment around the training. A section of pullup bars. A clear stretch of floor for handstand practice. Parallettes lined up along one wall. A space that was purpose-built for the way Shoshin taught.

The community at McLeod Street grew quickly. Classes hit 25 people a session, and the small space buzzed with energy. People weren't just showing up for a workout — they were showing up for each other. Regular faces became friends, then became part of the identity of the place. As one member put it: "Not your average gym. Fantastic community."

But 75 square metres has a ceiling, literally and otherwise. Within a year the class sizes were at capacity for the space, and the studio was bursting at the seams. They'd outgrown the space almost as quickly as they'd moved in. It was time for more room.

Grafton Street, CBD

2019 – 2026 · The final chapter

In 2019, Shoshin made the move to Level 2, 82 Grafton Street — upstairs inside the Mainstreet Arcade, right in the heart of Cairns CBD. It was a proper space: open plan, high ceilings, pull-up bars, and enough floor to run the kind of sessions the studio was always meant to run. For the next seven years, this would be home.

The Grafton Street era was where Shoshin grew from a small studio into something Cairns had never had before — and still hasn't since. A dedicated movement studio focused on calisthenics, handstands, and strength-based flexibility work. No one else in the city was doing it. No one else in the region was doing it. Shoshin was the go-to — the only place in Far North Queensland where you could walk in off the street and learn a structured handstand progression, build real gymnastic strength, or develop the kind of mobility that most people assumed required a lifetime of yoga.

At its peak, around 100 members were training regularly. Waitlists for new members stretched one to two months. On busy nights the studio would run two classes simultaneously, 30 people on the floor, and it worked — because the progressive system meant beginners and advanced athletes could train side by side, each working at their own edge.

The offering crystallised into three pillars: Strength, Mobility, and Skill. Strength classes built fitness through calisthenics, kettlebell work, ground movement, crawling, hanging, jumping, and locomotion sequences that were equal parts challenging and playful. Mobility classes drew from gymnastics, physiotherapy, yoga, and martial arts — chasing not just passive flexibility but the active control to use it. And the handstand program became the studio's signature: a structured, progressive method that took people from their first wall-supported kick-up to freestanding holds, shapes, presses, and beyond.

The results spoke for themselves. Dozens of members hit the benchmark to graduate from beginner handstand class — a 60-second freestanding hold. Dozens of everyday women and mothers built to five full range-of-motion chin-ups in a row. Members reached above double-bodyweight deadlifts. People who came in unable to touch their toes left doing full splits. The method worked, and the proof was on the floor every session.

The coaching team grew from within. Annina Strengell — a member since 2015, loyal, committed, and a natural in front of a class — stepped up to lead strength sessions and became one of the studio's cornerstones until the very end. Rossi Postler, warm and deeply skilled, took on handstand and mobility coaching, bringing over 500 hours of yoga training alongside the Shoshin method. Other coaches came and went over the years, but Annina and Rossi were the constants — the two Dave trusted to carry the standard.

And then there was Holly. She first joined Shoshin's kids classes at 12 years old, transitioned into the adult program, and over the years built up to a one-arm handstand and nine consecutive press-to-handstands — all under the Shoshin method. She went on to be accepted into a Bachelor of Circus Arts at the National Institute of Circus Arts (NICA) in Melbourne. The full pipeline: beginner kid to elite-level performer, developed entirely within the studio's walls.

The Cairns Post featured Shoshin twice over the years — recognition that what was happening upstairs in the Mainstreet Arcade was genuinely noteworthy. As Pesche put it: "Best movement gym in QLD. It's a no ego, intelligent and structured approach." Todd, who had worked at CrossFit gyms and yoga studios in Los Angeles, called Shoshin his "favourite gym of all" — praising the classes and noting that "Dave is extremely knowledgeable and knows exactly what modifications are appropriate for all bodies." And Patrick simply said: "Training at Shoshin Movement pretty much changed everything about me."

Behind the scenes, David's partner at the time, Hannah, helped manage the business side — administration, marketing, and the operational load that comes with running a growing studio.

The studio survived things that would have ended many small businesses. COVID lockdowns in 2020 shut the doors and forced a pivot to online classes. When they reopened, the community came back. Cyclones, floods, and the economic reality of running a niche fitness studio in a regional city tested the business year after year. The lights stayed on, the mats stayed clean, and the classes kept filling up.

What made Grafton Street special wasn't the equipment or the programming — it was what happened in the space between sets. The conversations before class. The encouragement during a difficult hold. The celebration when someone nailed something they'd been working on for months. Shoshin built the kind of community that most gyms put on their marketing but few actually create: a place where people genuinely knew each other, looked out for each other, and showed up as much for the people as for the training.

On 15 February 2026, after nearly seven years at Grafton Street and twelve years since that first class at Fusion, the studio held its final session. The mats were rolled up, the pull-up bars came down, and the space that had held thousands of classes, hundreds of handstand attempts, and countless small victories went quiet.

But Shoshin was never really about the space. It was about the idea — that you can always begin again, that the willingness to be a beginner is the bravest thing you can do, and that everyone deserves to move better. That idea lives on in every person who walked up those stairs and left a little stronger than when they arrived.

Still Moving

2026 – present · The method continues

The studio is gone, but the method isn't. Dave is now running Shoshin Strength and Shoshin Mobility classes at The Yoga School in Cairns — same coaching, same philosophy, new home.
